The explanation most scientists accept today was put forward in the eighteen-nineties by two scientists at Trinity College Dublin. Henry Dixon, a botanist, and John Joly, a physicist.
A key clue came from experiments in Germany. Tall stems that had been killed with poison kept drawing water up. The flow only stopped when the leaves were killed.
So the engine isn't in the trunk. It's in the leaves.
Inside every leaf, water evaporates from the wet walls of the cells, and the vapour escapes through tiny pores called stomata.
Those cell walls are full of pores about a hundred-thousandth of a millimetre across. And a water surface stretched across a pore that small can pull back with enormous force.
As water evaporates, it tugs on the water behind it.
And water molecules cling to each other, and to the walls of the pipes.
So the tug travels down an unbroken column of water, through the trunk, all the way to the roots.
The whole column rises like a rope pulled from the top.
It's called the cohesion-tension theory.
And it means the water inside a tree is in a very strange state.
Its pressure is below zero. Not low. Negative. A liquid being stretched.
In many trees, the water is being pulled about twenty times harder than the air presses on you.
Pure water can take it, because in the lab it can be stretched much harder before it snaps.
In nineteen sixty-five, scientists found a way to measure that tension, with a device now nicknamed the pressure bomb. Seal a cut twig in a chamber, raise the pressure until sap appears at the cut, and you've measured how hard the tree was pulling.
You can even watch it work. On a sunny day, a tree trunk shrinks, very slightly, as the tension squeezes it. At night, it swells back.
And the energy? The sun and the dry air do the work. The tree spends none of its own energy on the lift.
It took the best part of a century for the idea to win over its doubters, and a few scientists still argue the details.
But stretched water is risky water.
Pull too hard, and the column can snap. A bubble of air forms in a pipe, and the pipe fills with air. It's blocked.
Scientists first picked up the sound of this in the nineteen-sixties, as tiny clicks from thirsty plants. Today, ultrasonic sensors record them. In lab tests, each pulse matches a single bubble.
So trees are built to contain the damage.
Their pipes connect through tiny pits, and the thin membranes in those pits work like safety valves. Water gets through. Air doesn't.
In conifers, each pit even has a little plug that slams shut when the pipe next door fills with air.
But safety has a price. The safer the valve, the harder it is to push water through it.
And in a drought, the pull keeps getting stronger, until air starts forcing its way through the valves.
Pipe after pipe fills with air, and the tree's plumbing shuts down.
That's one of the main ways drought kills trees. Their water lines break.
Whether a tree can ever clear a blocked pipe is still debated. Mostly, it seems to grow new wood instead.
This design has one more consequence. It may set a limit on how tall a tree can grow.
