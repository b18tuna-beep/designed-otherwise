## 2. Attempt one: suck it up (~0:50 to 2:10)

First idea. Put a pump at the top and suck the water up, like a straw. [none]
People tried this for centuries. And in the sixteen-hundreds, pump makers kept hitting the same wall. [2.3]
Their suction pumps would not raise water past about eighteen braccia. Roughly ten or eleven metres. [2.3]
Galileo puzzled over the problem, and couldn't explain it. [2.3]
The answer came in the sixteen-forties from Evangelista Torricelli, Galileo's successor in Florence. [2.3, A1]
A suction pump doesn't really pull water at all. It lowers the pressure above the water, and the weight of the air outside pushes the water up the pipe. [2.2, 2.3]
And air can only push so hard. At sea level, it balances a column of water about ten metres tall. [2.1]
Even a perfect vacuum can't beat that. [2.2]
So suction has a hard ceiling. About ten metres. [2.1]
The tallest trees lift water more than ten times higher. [2.4]
Whatever a tree is doing, it isn't sucking. [2.4]

## 3. Attempt two: push from below (~2:10 to 3:20)

Next idea. Forget the top. Push from the bottom. [none]
And roots really can do this. [3.1]
Root cells pack minerals into their water pipes, water follows by osmosis, and pressure builds. Mostly at night, when the leaves are resting. [3.1]
You can see it. On a still, humid night, some plants push droplets out of the edges and tips of their leaves. It's called guttation, and it isn't dew. [3.2]
And in spring, a cut birch or grapevine will drip sap pushed up from its roots. [3.3]
Maple syrup, by the way, is a different story. Maple sap runs because of freezing and thawing inside the trunk, not because of the roots. [3.4]
But root pressure is weak. At best, a few storeys' worth of push. A small fraction of a tall tree. [3.5]
And when scientists measure the water inside a tree on a sunny day, it isn't being pushed at all. [3.7]
It's being pulled. [3.7]
Hold that thought. [none]

## 4. Attempt three: thin pipes (~3:20 to 4:20)

Third idea. Capillary action. [none]
Dip a thin glass tube in water, and the water climbs up it on its own. The narrower the tube, the higher it goes. [4.1]
And a tree is full of thin tubes. The wood that carries water, the xylem, is a bundle of microscopic pipes. [4.2]
But they're not thin enough. The narrowest are a few hundredths of a millimetre wide. The widest, about half a millimetre. [4.2]
In tubes that size, capillary action lifts water a few centimetres, a few metres at most. [4.3]
To reach a hundred metres by capillary action alone, even the thinnest pipes would need to be about thirty times narrower. [4.3]
So. Three good ideas. Three failures. [none]
And yet, the water gets there. [1.1]

## 5. Nature's answer: pull from the top (~4:20 to 6:40)

The explanation most scientists accept today was put forward in the eighteen-nineties by two scientists at Trinity College Dublin. Henry Dixon, a botanist, and John Joly, a physicist. [5.1]
A key clue came from experiments in Germany. Tall stems that had been killed with poison kept drawing water up. The flow only stopped when the leaves were killed. [5.2]
So the engine isn't in the trunk. It's in the leaves. [5.2, 5.3]
Inside every leaf, water evaporates from the wet walls of the cells, and the vapour escapes through tiny pores called stomata. [5.3]
Those cell walls are full of pores about a hundred-thousandth of a millimetre across. And a water surface stretched across a pore that small can hold back an enormous pull. [4.4]
As water evaporates, it tugs on the water behind it. [5.3]
And water molecules cling to each other, and to the walls of the pipes. [5.4]
So the tug travels down an unbroken column of water, through the trunk, all the way to the roots. [5.3]
The whole column rises like a rope pulled from the top. [5.4, 5.5]
It's called the cohesion-tension theory. [5.1]
And it means the water inside a tree is in a very strange state. [5.5]
Its pressure is below zero. Not low. Negative. A liquid being stretched. [5.5]
In many trees, that pull can reach about twenty times the pressure of the air around you, pulling the wrong way. [5.6]
Pure water can take it, because in the lab it can be stretched much harder before it snaps. [5.7]
In nineteen sixty-five, scientists measured that pull with a device now nicknamed the pressure bomb. Seal a cut twig in a chamber, raise the pressure until sap appears at the cut, and you've measured how hard the tree was pulling. [5.8]
You can even watch it work. On a sunny day, a tree trunk shrinks, very slightly, as the pull squeezes it. At night, it swells back. [5.9]
And the energy? The sun and the dry air do the work. The tree spends none of its own energy on the lift. [5.10, 1.6]
It took the best part of a century for the idea to win over its doubters, and a few scientists still argue the details. [5.11]

## 6. When the rope snaps (~6:40 to 8:00)

But stretched water is risky water. [5.5, 6.1]
Pull too hard, and the column can snap. A bubble of air forms in a pipe, and the pipe fills with air. It's blocked. [6.1]
Scientists first picked up the sound of this in the nineteen-sixties, as tiny clicks from thirsty plants. Today, ultrasonic sensors record them. In lab tests, each pulse matches a single bubble. [6.3]
So trees are built to contain the damage. [6.4]
Their pipes connect through tiny pits, and the thin membranes in those pits work like safety valves. Water gets through. Air doesn't. [6.4]
In conifers, each pit even has a little plug that slams shut when the pipe next door fills with air. [6.4]
But safety has a price. The safer the valve, the harder it is to push water through it. [6.4]
And in a drought, the pull keeps getting stronger, until air starts forcing its way through the valves. [6.1, 6.2]
Pipe after pipe fills with air, and the tree's plumbing shuts down. [6.2, 6.6]
That's one of the main ways drought kills trees. Their water lines break. [6.6]
Whether a tree can ever clear a blocked pipe is still debated. Mostly, it seems to grow new wood instead. [6.7]

## 7. The ceiling (~8:00 to 9:10)

This design has one more consequence. It may set a limit on how tall a tree can grow. [7.1]
Every metre of height adds more pull just to hold the water up. About one atmosphere for every ten metres. [7.2]
So the top of a tall tree is always the thirstiest part. [7.3]
In two thousand and four, a team of scientists climbed the tallest redwoods in California and measured their leaves from bottom to top. [7.1]
Near the top, the leaves were small, thick and tightly packed. Water-starved. [7.3]
Following that trend upward, they estimated redwoods could grow no taller than about a hundred and twenty-two to a hundred and thirty metres. [7.1]
That's a prediction from the data, not a law of nature. But a different team, studying a different tree, found a similar ceiling. [7.1, 7.5]
And the tallest tree known today, Hyperion, at about a hundred and sixteen metres, stands just under it. [1.1, 7.4]

## 8. Close (~9:10 to 9:45)

A big tree can move hundreds of litres of water on a summer day. [8.1]
And almost all of it simply evaporates, out through the leaves into the air. [8.2]
So the next time you walk past a tree on a sunny day, think about what's going on inside it. [none]
Columns of water, stretched tight, hanging from the leaves. [5.5]
Lifted by the sun, through pipes too wide to work, past a limit no pump can beat. [5.10, 4.3, 2.4]
