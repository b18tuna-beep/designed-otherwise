## Format: an engineering challenge
P1 was a paradox explained, P2 a detective story. P3 is a design brief that every obvious solution fails, then nature's answer.
"Lift water higher than a 30-storey building. No pump, no motor, no power bill, no moving parts. And you can't suck it up: suction stops at about ten metres." Each failed attempt teaches one piece of physics; the tree's answer uses all of them.

## Beats (draft, to be firmed up by the research)
1. **Cold open, the brief.** The challenge, then the reveal that a tree does it every sunny day. Hook on the viewer: a tree outside your window is doing it right now. Intro sting.
2. **Attempt 1: suck it up.** Why suction cannot lift water above ~10.3 m (atmospheric pressure holds up a 10 m column; the 17th-century well-pump problem, Torricelli). The tallest trees are 10x past that.
3. **Attempt 2: push from below.** Root pressure is real (guttation, sap in spring maples) but weak and absent when it matters: on a sunny day the water in a trunk is pulled, not pushed.
4. **Attempt 3: capillary action.** The pipes (xylem) are thin, but capillary rise in tubes that wide is centimetres to well under a metre.
5. **Nature's answer: pull from the top.** Evaporation from the leaves (through stomata) bends tiny water surfaces in the cell walls; those surfaces pull. Water molecules cling to each other (cohesion), so the whole column rises like a rope: the cohesion-tension theory (Dixon and Joly, 1894). The water is under tension, negative pressure, a stretched liquid. Evidence: the pressure chamber (Scholander, 1965) and trunks that measurably shrink during the day.
6. **The danger: when the rope snaps.** Too much tension and an air bubble breaks the column (cavitation/embolism), audible as ultrasonic clicks. The design that contains it: pits between conduits act like valves that seal off a failed pipe. Drought kills trees this way.
7. **The limit.** Height raises the tension at the top; the tallest redwoods' top leaves are water-starved, and the physics predicts a maximum height around 122-130 m (Koch et al. 2004). The tallest known tree (a coast redwood) is about 116 m.
8. **Scale and close.** How much water a big tree moves in a day; almost all of it evaporates (only a tiny share is used to build the tree). "The tallest pump on Earth has no moving parts" / Designed otherwise.
