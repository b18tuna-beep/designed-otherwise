## 1. Cold open (0:00 to 0:30)

Why does your refrigerator have to heat up to keep your food cold? [1.1]
It sounds backwards. [none]
But put your hand on the side of a running fridge, or near the grille at the bottom, and you'll often feel warmth. [1.1]
In a hot room, the sides of some models get hotter than fifty degrees Celsius. That's about a hundred and twenty Fahrenheit. [1.1]
That heat isn't a defect. It's the whole trick. [3.8]
And tucked away inside, right next to the coldest part of the machine, there's a second heater. One that switches on on purpose. [5.3]

## 2. Cold is not a thing (0:30 to 1:30)

Let's start with the way we talk about cold. [none]
We talk about cold as if it were a thing. Let the cold out. Keep the cold in. [none]
But there's no such substance. There is no cold to add. [2.2]
What exists is heat: energy that flows because of a difference in temperature. [2.2]
And on its own, heat only flows one way. From hotter to colder. [2.1]
A hot mug warms your hands. A cold drink never chills them by pushing cold in. Your hands are simply losing heat to it. [2.1, 2.2]
So a refrigerator doesn't make cold. It removes heat. Your milk is cold because heat has been taken out of it. [2.2]
Here's the problem. Your kitchen is warmer than the inside of your fridge. [none]
Getting heat out of the cold box and into the warm room means moving it in the one direction it will never go by itself. [2.1]
Physicists have a name for this rule. It's the Clausius statement of the second law of thermodynamics. Moving heat from a cold place to a warmer one takes work. [2.1]
So your fridge works like a heat pump. It spends electricity to push heat uphill, out of your food and into your kitchen. [2.3]

## 3. The loop (1:30 to 4:30)

To pull that off, it uses one fluid travelling around and around a sealed loop. The refrigerant. [3.1, 3.3]
Follow one drop of it all the way round, and the whole machine makes sense. [none]
The trick it relies on is boiling. [3.1]
A liquid's boiling point depends on pressure. Lower the pressure, and it boils at a lower temperature. Raise the pressure, and the boiling point climbs. [3.9]
Household refrigerants boil far below freezing, even at ordinary air pressure. [3.6]
Isobutane, one of the common ones, boils at about eleven degrees Fahrenheit. [3.6]
Now, the loop. Stop one is the evaporator, a coil inside the fridge. [3.1, 5.3]
Here the refrigerant is kept at low pressure, so it boils, and boiling soaks up heat. [3.1]
That heat comes from inside the fridge. From the air, the shelves, last night's leftovers, and the warmth that keeps creeping in through the walls and door. This is where it all ends up. [3.1, 2.2, 3.11]
Stop two is the compressor. [3.2]
The cold, low pressure vapor flows into it, and the compressor squeezes it into a high pressure vapor that is much hotter. Hot enough to be well above room temperature. [3.2]
This is where the electricity does its main job. The compressor is doing the work. [2.3]
Stop three is the condenser. [3.3]
Depending on the design, that's a coil on the back, tubing hidden inside the side walls, or a coil in a compartment at the bottom with a fan blowing across it. [1.1]
The vapor arriving here is hotter than your kitchen, so heat flows out of it into the room. The easy direction. [4.1, 3.3]
As it loses heat, the vapor condenses back into a liquid, still under high pressure. [3.3]
Stop four is the expansion device. In a typical household fridge it's a capillary tube, a long, very thin tube that restricts the flow. [3.4]
It holds back the high pressure side from the low pressure side. Past it, the pressure drops, the boiling point drops with it, and the refrigerant starts to boil again. [3.4, 3.9]
In many fridges this tube is soldered to the line carrying cold vapor back to the compressor, so the warm liquid hands some of its heat to that vapor on the way. [3.5]
And then the refrigerant is back in the evaporator, and the loop starts again. [3.4, 3.1]
Now do the accounting. Energy is conserved. [3.8]
So the heat the fridge dumps into your kitchen equals all the heat it pulled out of the inside, plus the electrical energy it used to do it. [3.8]
On a tough standard test, a household compressor moves roughly one point four to two units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses. [3.8]

## 4. Why it must run hot (4:30 to 5:30)

So here's the missing context to the title. [none]
Heat only flows downhill. For heat to leave the condenser on its own, the refrigerant inside has to be hotter than the air in your kitchen. [4.1]
That's the compressor's whole job: push it well above room temperature. [4.1]
A condenser that stayed at room temperature couldn't get rid of anything. [4.1]
Dust, lint and pet hair on the condenser block the airflow and make it run hotter, so the compressor has to work harder and longer. [4.2]
Hotpoint says to clean the coils at least once a year. Some models don't need routine cleaning at all. [4.2]
Space matters too. Manufacturers specify air gaps, and they vary a lot. [4.3]
One G E side by side asks for just an eighth of an inch at the sides. Samsung recommends at least five centimetres, about two inches, at the sides and back. [4.3]
And putting a fridge next to an oven, a dishwasher, a heating vent, or in direct sunlight makes it harder for it to shed heat. Energy guides and installation instructions say to avoid it. [4.4]

## 5. The second heater (5:30 to 7:30)

Now for that second heater. [5.3]
The evaporator stays below freezing. And the air inside your fridge carries moisture, from your food, and from warm, humid kitchen air that rushes in every time you open the door. [5.1]
That moisture condenses on the cold coil and freezes. Frost. [5.1]
Frost conducts heat very poorly. A layer of it insulates the coil and chokes the airflow, so the fridge cools worse and wastes energy. [5.2]
A frost free fridge solves it with an electric heater, mounted on or just below the evaporator. Often a metal rod, or a heating element inside a glass tube. [5.3]
While it's on, it can draw about three times as much power as the compressor. [5.8]
In the two service manuals we checked, the heater drew about four hundred and fifty to four hundred and seventy watts. The compressor, roughly a hundred and twenty to a hundred and sixty. [5.8]
But the heater only runs for minutes at a time. [5.8]
So when does it switch on? It goes by how long the compressor has been running. [5.4]
Older timers start a defrost about every eight hours of running. Newer controls adapt: keep the doors shut, and some models wait a hundred hours or more. [5.4]
Then there's the water. [5.6]
In many fridges, the melted frost drains through a tube to a shallow pan down near the condenser. [5.6]
There, the condenser's warmth, and in many models its fan, evaporate the water back into your kitchen. [5.6]
The fridge uses its own waste heat to get rid of its own meltwater. [5.6]
And there's more deliberate heat. Many fridges warm the edges of the cabinet where the doors seal, either with a loop of warm refrigerant tubing or with small electric heaters. [5.7]
The point is to stop condensation forming on the outside. Some models even have an energy saver switch that turns that heater off. [5.7]
So the box that keeps your food cold has warm edges around its doors, and a heater mounted right next to its coldest coil. [5.7, 5.3]

## 6. The bill (7:30 to 8:30)

All of that shows up on your electricity bill. The good news is how small it has become. [6.1]
A new, efficient refrigerator uses a few hundred kilowatt hours a year. [6.1]
One eighteen cubic foot top freezer model on the ENERGY STAR list is rated at three hundred and sixty three kilowatt hours a year. That's about ten percent under the federal limit for its size. [6.1]
In nineteen seventy four, the average new fridge used more than eighteen hundred. New fridges use roughly a quarter of the energy of mid seventies models, and they hold more. [6.2]
Which brings us to the old advice about the open fridge door. [none]
Leaving it open won't cool your kitchen. [6.3]
Every bit of heat it pulls out goes straight back into the same room through the condenser, along with heat from the electricity it uses. So the room ends up warmer. [6.3]
All you get is a brief puff of cold air at the door. [6.3]

## 7. Close (8:30 to 9:00)

So your fridge doesn't make cold. It moves heat. [2.2, 2.3]
Out of the cold box, around a loop of boiling and squeezing, and into your kitchen, with a little extra added on the way. [3.8]
Much of that warmth on the side of the cabinet is heat that came from inside your fridge. [3.8, 6.3]
