Right now, if your hearing is normal, there's a good chance your ears are making a sound. [5.4]

Not ringing. Usually far too quiet to notice. A faint, steady tone, made inside your ear, and slipping out into your ear canal. [5.4, 5.5]
It sounds like a myth. But play a sound into a healthy ear, and it answers so reliably that hospitals count on it. [1.3, 5.1]

## 2. The microphone we think we have (0:35 to 2:05)

Here's the ear most of us picture. [none]
Sound travels down the ear canal and makes the eardrum vibrate. [2.1]
Three tiny bones, the hammer, the anvil and the stirrup, pass that vibration on to the entrance of the cochlea. [2.1]
The stirrup, the last of the three, is the smallest bone in the human body. About three millimetres long. [2.5]
Simple enough. A microphone with some plumbing. [none]
But why the bones at all? Why not let the air push on the cochlea directly? [2.2]
Because the cochlea is full of fluid. And when sound in air hits fluid, about ninety-nine point nine percent of its energy simply bounces off. [2.2]
Like shouting at someone underwater. [none]
So the middle ear concentrates the push. The eardrum is roughly fifteen to twenty times bigger than the footplate of the stirrup. The same force, focused onto a tiny spot. Like a stiletto heel. [2.3]
The little bones add a bit of leverage on top. [2.3]
Together, they raise the pressure reaching the cochlea many times over. [2.4]
And here's an odd detail. Birds and reptiles have just one ear bone, the stirrup. [2.7]
Our other two, the hammer and the anvil, began as the bones of the jaw joint in our distant, reptile-like ancestors. Over many millions of years, those old jaw bones shrank and ended up carrying sound instead. [2.7]
So far, this is all passive. Bones and levers, handing vibration along. [none]
The surprise is in what they hand it to. [none]

## 3. A piano, rolled up (2:05 to 3:55)

Inside your skull sits the cochlea. A fluid-filled tube, coiled like a snail shell, about two and a half turns. [3.1]
Unrolled, it would be only about three centimetres long. [3.2]
Running along it is a strip called the basilar membrane. And it changes from one end to the other. [3.3]
Near the entrance, the base, it's narrow and stiff. At the far end, the apex, it's wider and much floppier. [3.3]
So each spot along it responds most to one pitch. High notes near the base. Low notes near the apex. [3.4]
Pitch is laid out along the spiral, a bit like the keys of a piano. From about twenty vibrations a second to about twenty thousand. [3.4]
When a sound arrives, a ripple runs along the membrane from the base, grows to a peak at the place tuned to that pitch, then quickly dies away. A travelling wave. [3.5]
The man who showed this was Georg von Békésy. He won the nineteen sixty-one Nobel Prize for it. [3.6]
And he worked it out largely with cochleas from human cadavers. [3.6]
That turned out to matter. [none]
Because the dead ears missed something. [3.7]
Later measurements in living animals showed the membrane responding far more strongly to quiet sounds, and far more sharply tuned, than anything in Békésy's cadavers. [3.7]
And that extra sensitivity faded when the animal died. [3.7]
Something in a living cochlea was adding energy. [3.7]
Someone had predicted exactly this. In nineteen forty-eight, a young Cambridge physicist, Thomas Gold, argued that the ear must contain an amplifier. Something actively pumping energy in to overcome the damping of the fluid. [3.8]
Hardly anyone took it up. The idea was largely forgotten for about thirty years. [3.8]

## 4. The amplifier (3:55 to 6:00)

To find Gold's amplifier, look at the hair cells. [none]
The cochlea has two kinds. One row of inner hair cells, about three and a half thousand of them. And usually three rows of outer hair cells, roughly three times as many. [4.1]
Each is topped with a tuft of tiny hairs called stereocilia. [4.1]
When the membrane moves, the stereocilia bend. That opens tiny channels at their tips, charged particles rush in, and the cell makes an electrical signal for the hearing nerve. [4.3]
Now here's the strange part. About ninety-five percent of the fibres in your hearing nerve carry signals from the inner hair cells. [4.2]
Only about five percent connect to the outer hair cells. [4.2]
So three-quarters of your hair cells barely talk to the brain. [4.1, 4.2]
What are they for? [none]
In nineteen eighty-five, William Brownell found a big clue. He was a hearing researcher from the University of Florida, working in a lab in Geneva, Switzerland. He and his colleagues took outer hair cells out of animal ears and applied a current. The cells physically changed length. Shorter one way. Longer the other. [4.4, 4.4a]
They're not just sensors. They're motors. [4.4, 4.7]
In two thousand, researchers at Northwestern University found the protein that drives them. They named it prestin, after the musical marking presto. Fast. [4.5]
And fast they are. In lab tests on isolated cells, outer hair cells kept changing length in step with the signal tens of thousands of times a second. Faster than the highest pitch you can hear. [4.6]
By pushing on the membrane in time with the sound, they boost the faintest vibrations roughly a hundredfold, and sharpen the tuning. [4.7]
They turn up whispers much more than shouts. [4.7]
The proof came from mice. Mice engineered without prestin lost that movement, and became forty to sixty decibels less sensitive, even though their hair cells could still sense motion. [4.8]
Gold was right. The ear isn't a microphone. It has an amplifier built in. [3.8, 4.7]

## 5. The ear talks back (6:00 to 7:20)

And amplifiers have a side effect. [5.2]
In nineteen seventy-eight, David Kemp, a physicist working at a London ear hospital, played clicks into people's ears and recorded what came back. [5.1]
A few milliseconds later, a faint sound came back out of the ear. Made inside the cochlea. [5.1]
It was there in every normal ear he tested. And missing in ears with cochlear deafness. [5.1]
The same outer-hair-cell activity that boosts incoming sound sends a tiny wave back out, through the middle ear to the eardrum, where a sensitive microphone can pick it up. [5.2]
These are otoacoustic emissions. And that's what the newborn test is listening for. [1.3, 5.2]
A clear response means the outer hair cells, and the middle ear, are working. [1.4]
It's a check of the ear, not the hearing nerve. So when the nerve is the question, babies get a different test that measures how the nerve and brainstem respond. [1.5]
In twenty twenty, about ninety-eight out of every hundred babies born in the United States were screened. [1.1]
And many healthy ears go further. With no sound going in at all, they give off faint, steady tones. Spontaneous otoacoustic emissions. [5.4]
More than half of people with normal hearing have at least one. Newborns included. And they're more common in women than in men. [5.4]
They're very quiet, usually far too faint for you to notice. [5.5]

## 6. The price of an amplifier (7:20 to 8:40)

An amplifier this good comes at a cost. [none]
Long or repeated exposure to sound at or above eighty-five decibels can damage hearing. The louder the sound, the less time it takes. [6.1]
And outer hair cells, the amplifiers, are among the first cells loud noise destroys. [6.2]
High pitches usually go first. The most hair-cell loss shows up at the high-frequency end, the base of the cochlea. [6.5]
Lose the outer hair cells, and you lose the amplifier. In animal studies, hearing became about fifty decibels less sensitive, and the sharp tuning blurred. [6.6]
Quiet sounds vanish. Sounds close in pitch become harder to tell apart. [6.6]
And here's the hard part. In humans, and in mammals generally, lost hair cells don't grow back. The damage is permanent. [6.4]
Birds are different. In nineteen eighty-eight, two research teams showed that birds regrow their hair cells after noise damage. [6.4]
We can't. [6.4]
For long days around loud sound, the US workplace safety agency recommends a limit of eighty-five decibels averaged over an eight-hour shift. [6.1]
