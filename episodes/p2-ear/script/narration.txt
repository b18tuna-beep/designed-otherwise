The surprise is in what they hand it to.
Inside your skull sits the cochlea. A fluid-filled tube, coiled like a snail shell, about two and a half turns.
Unrolled, it would be only about three centimetres long.
Running along it is a strip called the basilar membrane. And it changes from one end to the other.
Near the entrance, the base, it's narrow and stiff. At the far end, the apex, it's wider and much floppier.
So each spot along it responds most to one pitch. High notes near the base. Low notes near the apex.
Pitch is laid out along the spiral, a bit like the keys of a piano. From about twenty vibrations a second to about twenty thousand.
When a sound arrives, a ripple runs along the membrane from the base, grows to a peak at the place tuned to that pitch, then quickly dies away. A travelling wave.
The man who showed this was Georg von Békésy. He won the nineteen sixty-one Nobel Prize for it.
And he worked it out largely with cochleas from human cadavers.
That turned out to matter.
Because the dead ears missed something.
Later measurements in living animals showed the membrane responding far more strongly to quiet sounds, and far more sharply tuned, than anything in Békésy's cadavers.
And that extra sensitivity faded when the animal died.
Something in a living cochlea was adding energy.
Someone had predicted exactly this. In nineteen forty-eight, a young Cambridge physicist, Thomas Gold, argued that the ear must contain an amplifier. Something actively pumping energy in to overcome the damping of the fluid.
Hardly anyone took it up. The idea was largely forgotten for about thirty years.
To find Gold's amplifier, look at the hair cells.
The cochlea has two kinds. One row of inner hair cells, about three and a half thousand of them. And usually three rows of outer hair cells, roughly three times as many.
Each is topped with a tuft of tiny hairs called stereocilia.
When the membrane moves, the stereocilia bend. That opens tiny channels at their tips, charged particles rush in, and the cell makes an electrical signal for the hearing nerve.
Now here's the strange part. About ninety-five percent of the fibres in your hearing nerve carry signals from the inner hair cells.
Only about five percent connect to the outer hair cells.
So three-quarters of your hair cells barely talk to the brain.
What are they for?
In nineteen eighty-five, William Brownell found a big clue. He was a hearing researcher from the University of Florida, working in a lab in Geneva, Switzerland. He and his colleagues took outer hair cells out of animal ears and applied a current. The cells physically changed length. Shorter one way. Longer the other.
They're not just sensors. They're motors.
In two thousand, researchers at Northwestern University found the protein that drives them. They named it prestin, after the musical marking presto. Fast.
And fast they are. In lab tests on isolated cells, outer hair cells kept changing length in step with the signal tens of thousands of times a second. Faster than the highest pitch you can hear.
By pushing on the membrane in time with the sound, they boost the faintest vibrations roughly a hundredfold, and sharpen the tuning.
They turn up whispers much more than shouts.
The proof came from mice. Mice engineered without prestin lost that movement, and became forty to sixty decibels less sensitive, even though their hair cells could still sense motion.
Gold was right. The ear isn't a microphone. It has an amplifier built in.
And amplifiers have a side effect.
In nineteen seventy-eight, David Kemp, a physicist working at a London ear hospital, played clicks into people's ears and recorded what came back.
A few milliseconds later, a faint sound came back out of the ear. Made inside the cochlea.
It was there in every normal ear he tested. And missing in ears with cochlear deafness.
The same outer-hair-cell activity that boosts incoming sound sends a tiny wave back out, through the middle ear to the eardrum, where a sensitive microphone can pick it up.
These are otoacoustic emissions. And that's what the newborn test is listening for.
A clear response means the outer hair cells, and the middle ear, are working.
It's a check of the ear, not the hearing nerve. So when the nerve is the question, babies get a different test that measures how the nerve and brainstem respond.

And many healthy ears go further. With no sound going in at all, they give off faint, steady tones. Spontaneous otoacoustic emissions.
More than half of people with normal hearing have at least one. Newborns included. And they're more common in women than in men.
They're very quiet, usually far too faint for you to notice.

An amplifier this good comes at a cost.
Long or repeated exposure to sound at or above eighty-five decibels can damage hearing. The louder the sound, the less time it takes.
And outer hair cells, the amplifiers, are among the first cells loud noise destroys.
High pitches usually go first. The most hair-cell loss shows up at the high-frequency end, the base of the cochlea.
Lose the outer hair cells, and you lose the amplifier. In animal studies, hearing became about fifty decibels less sensitive, and the sharp tuning blurred.
Quiet sounds vanish. Sounds close in pitch become harder to tell apart.
And here's the hard part. In humans, and in mammals generally, lost hair cells don't grow back. The damage is permanent.
Birds are different. In nineteen eighty-eight, two research teams showed that birds regrow their hair cells after noise damage.
We can't.
