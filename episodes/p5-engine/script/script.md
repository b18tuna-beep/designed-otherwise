## 1. Cold open (0:00 to ~0:35)

Next time you're stopped at a red light, listen to your engine. [none]
(1 s beat)
It's still turning, around seven hundred times a minute. [1.1]
Every engine idles a little differently, but that's nearly twelve turns every second. [1.1]
And in each cylinder, only one stroke in every four actually pushes. [1.4]

## 2. Stroke one: intake (~0:35 to ~2:05)

Here's our cylinder. [none]
A piston, a connecting rod, a crankshaft. And on the end of the crank, a heavy wheel called the flywheel. Keep an eye on it. [5.2, 7.3]
Up top, two valves: one lets air and fuel in, the other lets burnt gas out. Between them sits the spark plug. [2.1, 6.1, 4.1]
One full cycle takes two turns of the crankshaft. Seven hundred and twenty degrees. [1.2]
Four strokes, half a turn each. [2.2]
At this speed, each degree of that turn lasts about a quarter of a thousandth of a second. [1.3]
Stroke one: intake. [2.1]
The intake valve is open, and the piston falls. [2.1]
Here's the first surprise. The piston doesn't really suck anything in. [2.1]
As it falls, it lowers the pressure inside the cylinder, and the air outside, at higher pressure, pushes the charge in through the open valve. [2.1]
But at idle, there's a catch. [2.5]
The throttle, the valve your accelerator pedal opens, is partly closed. So the engine has to pull its air in against a restriction. [2.5]
That's called throttling, and it's one main reason a gasoline engine is less efficient when it's only lightly loaded. It adds to what engineers call pumping losses. [2.5]
And notice what's turning the crank right now. [1.4]
Not fuel. The flywheel. Its momentum is pulling the piston down. [1.4, 5.1]
Watch the valves, too. [2.3]
They don't snap open and shut exactly at the ends of the strokes. [2.3]
The intake valve opened a little before the piston reached the top, and it will close well after the piston has passed the bottom. [2.3]
They're worked by a camshaft, which turns at half the speed of the crank, because each valve only opens once every two turns. [2.4]
Clock: four hundredths of a second. One stroke done, and nothing has been made yet. [1.3, 1.4]

## 3. Stroke two: compression (~2:05 to ~3:35)

Stroke two: compression. [2.2]
The intake valve closes, and the piston climbs, squeezing the mixture into the small space at the top. [2.3, 3.1]
How hard it squeezes is called the compression ratio: the volume at the bottom, divided by the space that's left at the top. [3.1]
In a typical gasoline car engine, that's roughly ten to one, up to about twelve to one. [3.2]
Squeeze harder, and you get better efficiency. [3.3]
So why stop there? [3.2]
Because squeezing the mixture makes it hotter. [3.3]
Push it past a limit, and the unburned mixture can ignite by itself. That's knock. [3.3, 3.4]
The sudden release sends pressure waves through the cylinder. That's the knocking sound, and in severe cases it can damage the engine. [3.4]
A few engines push the limit. Mazda's Skyactiv G reached fourteen to one. [3.2]
And this is where the number on the fuel pump comes in. [3.5]
Octane isn't a measure of how much energy is in the fuel. [3.5]
It measures how well the fuel resists igniting by itself under compression. [3.5]
It's tested in a special engine that raises its compression until the fuel knocks, and compared against two reference fuels: iso-octane, rated one hundred, and heptane, rated zero. [3.6]
In the United States, regular is generally eighty-seven, and premium ninety-one to ninety-four. [3.8]
Higher compression needs fuel that resists knock. That's the trade-off. [3.3]
And this stroke, too, is paid for by the flywheel. [1.4]
Clock: nine hundredths of a second. Half the cycle gone. Still no work done. [1.3, 1.4]

## 4. The spark comes early (~3:35 to ~4:50)

Now, something happens before the piston reaches the top. [4.1]
The spark fires, a few tens of degrees early. [4.1]
Why early? Because burning takes time. [4.2]
The spark doesn't set off an explosion. [4.3]
It starts a tiny, hot kernel, which grows into a flame. Wrinkled by the swirling air, it sweeps outward through the mixture until it has all burned. [4.3]
In MIT's figures for a modern engine, half the mixture has burned only after about twenty-five degrees of crank rotation. [4.4]
So the spark is timed to put the pressure peak just after the top, where the piston can use it. [4.2]
Fire too late, and the heat arrives too far into the stroke to be turned into work. [4.2]
Fire too early, and the engine wastes heat and makes knock more likely. [4.2]
Because the abnormal, much faster burn is knock: the mixture ahead of the flame igniting by itself. [4.3]
Modern engines listen for it, and delay the spark to stop it, at some cost in power and fuel economy. [3.7]

## 5. Stroke three: power (~4:50 to ~6:05)

Stroke three: power. [5.1]
The burning gas raises the pressure, and pushes the piston down. [5.1]
This, and only this, is where the engine does its work. [5.1]
Half a turn. About four hundredths of a second. [1.3]
And the flywheel stores some of that push, and gives it back over the next three strokes. That evens out the pulses. [5.2]
A single cylinder fires only once every two turns. [5.3]
So engines share the job. [5.3]
In a four-cylinder engine, a power stroke starts every hundred and eighty degrees. [5.3]
A common firing order is one, three, four, two. So at any moment, each cylinder is on a different stroke: one pushing, while the others breathe in, squeeze, and breathe out. [5.4]
Six cylinders, a power stroke every hundred and twenty degrees. Eight, every ninety. [5.3]
The more cylinders, the more those pushes overlap, and the smoother the engine runs. [5.3]
Clock: thirteen hundredths of a second. [1.3]

## 6. Clock stopped: whose cycle is it? (~6:05 to ~7:25)

Let's stop the clock for a moment. [none]
This four-stroke cycle is often called the Otto cycle. [7.7]
Nikolaus Otto was a German gas-engine builder. With his partner, Eugen Langen, he'd already won a gold medal at the eighteen sixty-seven Paris exhibition, for an earlier gas engine. [7.3, 7.6]
In eighteen seventy-six, Otto built an engine on the four-stroke principle. It was an immediate commercial success. [7.2, 7.6]
His American patent, from eighteen seventy-seven, describes the same four strokes, with the flywheel's momentum driving the piston back. [7.3, 7.4]
But Otto wasn't the first to describe it. [7.1]
In eighteen sixty-two, a French engineer, Alphonse Beau de Rochas, had set out the four-stroke cycle on paper. He never built an engine. [7.1]
In eighteen eighty-six, after that earlier description was brought to light, Otto's German patent was revoked. [7.5]
His patent was really about a layered charge of fuel. It says that instead of an explosion ensuing, the flame passes gradually from one particle to another. [7.3, 7.4]
Today's engines don't use his layered charge. But the burn is still a spreading flame, not a bang. [4.3]
Back to the clock. [none]

## 7. Stroke four: exhaust (~7:25 to ~8:40)

Stroke four: exhaust. [6.1]
The exhaust valve opened just before the bottom of the power stroke, to let the pressure drop. It costs a little work, but it's a net gain. [6.1]
Now the piston rises, and pushes the burnt gas out. [6.1]
Near the top, for a short moment, both valves are open together. Engineers call it valve overlap. [2.3]
And once again, it's the flywheel doing the work. [1.4]
So here's the bill. [6.2]
Of all the energy in the fuel, only about a fifth ends up moving a typical gasoline car. [6.2]
The U.S. Department of Energy puts it between twelve and thirty percent, depending on how it's driven. [6.2]
Most of the rest is lost in the engine itself, as heat. About sixty percent, out through the exhaust and the cooling system. [6.3]
Even at the crankshaft, before the gearbox and the tires, a gasoline engine turns roughly a third of its fuel energy into work, at best. [6.4]
Clock: seventeen hundredths of a second. [1.3]

## 8. Close (~8:40 to ~9:10)

One cycle. [1.2]
Two turns of the crank, four strokes, and only one of them pushing. [1.2, 1.4]
Three strokes out of every four, each cylinder is carried, by a heavy spinning wheel and by the cylinders next to it. [1.4, 5.2]
And then it all happens again. About six times a second, in every cylinder, while you wait for the light to change. [1.1, 1.3]
