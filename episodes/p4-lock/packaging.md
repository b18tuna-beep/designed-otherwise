Takeaways:
- **The mechanism slot is taken and won't be dislodged.** Jared Owen's 3-minute clip owns "how does a pin tumbler lock work". Do not title or describe P4 as a mechanism explainer. Win suggested/browse by being the "why it was designed this way" video that viewers watch after it.
- **Story works at 14 minutes.** Thoughty2's 1851 video reached 1.4M (V) with plain narration. That is a proof of demand for Bramah vs Hobbs, so the story is safe, but it is also the one most likely to be compared. Our edge: we connect it to the lock in the viewer's door.
- **The master key gap looks open.** The searches I ran returned articles, forum threads and locksmith pages, with no large video on "the second key that opens your door". That fits the brief's weakest-served beat and gives the most self-relevant hook.
- **"Unpickable" is the proven click word, "how to pick" is the crowded one.** Stuff Made Here and LockPickingLawyer show the audience for "claimed unbeatable, then beaten". Our no-tutorial line means we use the claim and the result, never the method.
- **Nobody found frames it as "the lock never reads your key".** Mechanism videos say the key lifts the pins; none that I found say the lock cannot see the key's shape. That is our unique sentence.

## Facts the packaging leans on (flag for the fact checker)

- Bramah challenge: 200 guineas, shop window, Piccadilly, since 1790 (Mental Floss, 99% Invisible). The lock itself dates to 1784. Hobbs worked on it in a room above the shop. **Sources disagree on the effort**: 51 hours over 16 days (Mental Floss) vs about 52 hours over 14 days (99% Invisible). Do not put a number in a title or hook until the dossier settles it.
- The Bramah lock and the Chubb Detector are not pin tumbler locks. Titles must not say your door lock was the one picked in 1851. The accurate line is that the "unpickable" claim failed, and the same principle follows every lock since.
- Master keying: two shear points per pin position, so a 6-pin cylinder can be operated by up to 2^6 = 64 keys in principle (Wikipedia). Blaze 2003 is conceptual only.
- "4,000-year-old": the brief states it. I could not confirm the date on a fetched page, so the dossier must source it before any title uses the number.

### Top 3, ranked

1. **"Look at Your Front Door Key. The Lock Never Does." (49)**
   Same pattern as P1's winner (a direct, self-test command), but with a twist, since it asks the viewer to look at something while the second sentence breaks a belief. It is concrete (a key in your pocket), it threatens the belief that a lock reads a key, it stays under 50 characters so nothing truncates on mobile, and the cold open says exactly this in its first line. It also contains "lock", "key" and "front door" for search. Weak point: "The Lock Never Does" could be read as slightly cryptic. That is acceptable since the thumbnail resolves it.
2. **"The Second Key That Opens Your Front Door" (41)**
   The most self-relevant gap and the least covered by video on the topic (see takeaways). Fear is mild and factual. Pays off in the cold open's last line and in beat 3. Paired with thumbnail B (two keys, one lock) it is the natural Test & Compare partner for #1, since it tests a different idea, not a different style. Risk: "Your" overclaims, so the narration must say "in many buildings" and show how common it is; the brief already does.
3. **"In 1851, an American Opened the 'Unpickable' Lock" (49)**
   The only story-led title, aimed at the history and "unpickable" audience already proven by Thoughty2 and Stuff Made Here. Ranked third because it has no viewer relevance and competes head-on with a 1.4M video. Use it as the retitle option if the first two get weak CTR in the first week (the packaging playbook allows retitling).

Recommendation: ship #1 with thumbnail A, and test thumbnail A against thumbnail B in Test & Compare. If CTR is weak after the first 48 hours, retitle to #2 or #3.

## 3. Thumbnail concepts

Rules applied: built on a 3D hero frame, one idea, max 3 words, readable at 160 px, text never repeats the paired title. Palette: dark slate, chalk white, violet for the working part. No arrowheads. Heat and electricity colours are not used.

**A. "The key it never sees" (pairs with title #1)**
- Image: Blender cutaway of the lock in profile. A chalk-white key is half inserted. Five pin stacks, with the working splits glowing violet and all landing on one thin violet shear line. The key's outline is deliberately dull and slightly out of focus, the pins sharp.
- Text: **JUST HEIGHTS** (2 words).
- Why it clicks: the title says the lock never looks at the key, and the image shows what it does look at. The viewer sees the pins, not the key, which makes the title feel true before they click. Highest contrast of the four at small size.

**B. "Two keys, one lock" (pairs with title #2, and is the Test & Compare partner)**
- Image: a single cutaway lock with two different keys side by side in front of it: one chalk, one violet outlined. Both keys are shown with a violet line to the same shear line. The two keys have clearly different cut patterns.
- Text: **BOTH WORK** (2 words).
- Why it clicks: it is an instant visual contradiction, since two different keys should not both fit one lock. It makes the second-key idea visible in one second and does not need the title to be read. Works at 160 px because the two keys read as a pair of shapes.

**C. "The shop-window challenge" (pairs with title #4)**
- Image: 3D lock in a lit shop-window frame, a chalk plaque beside it with the price. Pin stacks glowing violet inside.
- Text: **200 GUINEAS** (2 words).
- Why it clicks: a concrete prize is a story hook. A weaker click for a general audience, so it is a retitle thumbnail, not a launch one.

**D. "Numbers on a key" (pairs with title #2 or #1 as a third test)**
- Image: extreme close-up of a key blade whose cut depths are drawn as five violet bars rising from the teeth.
- Text: **5 NUMBERS** (2 words).
- Why it clicks: it makes the key look like a code. Good, but "5 numbers" slightly misrepresents the video if the lock has six pins, so use only if the final script says five.

**Mark for YouTube Test & Compare: A and B.** They differ in idea (one key, what the lock reads, vs two keys, who else has access), not just styling. That follows the "make two that differ in idea" rule from the playbook.

## 4. First 15 seconds

The brief's beat 1 is already in the right shape (viewer, the key in the pocket, a 1 s beat, the second-key sting). Three alternatives, written to be spoken by the British narrator, about 38 words each.

**Hook 1, the command** (best match for title #1)
"Take your front door key out of your pocket. Look at it. Now know this: the lock in your door has never looked at it. [1 s silence] It doesn't check the shape. It checks one thing, whether a row of tiny gaps lines up. And in many buildings, it will also accept a key you have never seen."

**Hook 2, the landlord**
"Somebody, somewhere, owns a key that opens your front door, and you've never met them. Your lock doesn't mind. It was never checking who the key belongs to, or even what it looks like. [1 s] It checks something much smaller. Here's what it actually reads."

**Hook 3, the challenge**
"In 1851, a London shop had a lock in its window with a prize for anyone who could open it. For sixty years, nobody could. Then an American walked in. And the same flaw he used is in the lock on your front door."

Check on Hook 3: "sixty years" depends on the sourced dates (61 years from the 1784 lock vs "70-plus" from the 1790 challenge), and "the same flaw" overstates since Bramah is not a pin tumbler. It is the weakest and needs rewording before use.

**Pick: Hook 1.** It is the only one that makes the title's second sentence happen on screen in the first 6 seconds, it is about the viewer, it plants the master key open loop in the last line, and it needs no sourced number, which makes it the safest for the fact checker. Add the visual on "Look at it": a macro of the key teeth, so the shot matches the title and thumbnail A.

## 6. Retention notes

Where this topic loses viewers and what to do:

- **0:00 to 0:20: the "I already know this" exit.** The Jared Owen audience thinks it knows a pin tumbler. Do not show the lock being operated by the key in a standard way in the first minute. Hook 1 spends this time on the belief break (the lock never looks at the key), which is new even to people who have seen the mechanism.
- **0:40 to 2:30, the pin stacks (beat 2): the tutorial trough.** This is the highest drop risk, since it is closest to the existing 3-minute video. Keep it under 90 seconds of mechanism, and put a pattern interrupt every 20 to 25 seconds: a wrong key that blocks the plug at about 1:00, then a "same lock, different key shape, same heights" reveal that proves the title. Ask a question at each step, not a description.
- **Open loops to plant early, pay late.** (1) Cold open: "a second key you have never seen". Pay at beat 3 (about 2:30), then deepen with the 64-key figure. (2) At the end of beat 2: "this design has a flaw that was found in 1851", paid at beat 5. (3) At beat 4: "the Bramah lock sat in a window for decades, and the man who opened it was not a thief". Pay at beat 5. (4) In beat 6: "the answer to why every pin lock can be beaten is a manufacturing detail, not a clever trick", paid in the same beat, then re-opened at beat 7 with "but most burglars never touch the lock". Pay it in the closing bargain.
- **Beat 4, history (about 4:00).** Date lists lose viewers. Make it a visual timeline that moves, not a list, and give Yale a human beat (father and son, one idea, one cylinder). Cut any date that does not change what the viewer thinks.
- **Beat 5, 1851 (about 5:00).** The strongest story, so let it breathe, but place a mid-beat interrupt at the reveal that the "unpickable" challenge sat for decades. Keep to sourced numbers and note that the effort figure differs between sources if it appears.
- **Beat 6, tolerances (about 6:30), the safety line.** The risk is both a retention dip and a policy edge. Keep it conceptual with the exaggerated animation only, no tools or sequence. A line like "this is why, and it is the only version of this we will show" turns the constraint into a trust beat.
- **Beat 7, answers (about 8:00).** Start with the twist (most break-ins force the door) as a question, then answer, rather than listing security pins and grades. List only if each item gets one image and one sentence. Source the burglary claim or cut it, since I found no statistic on a fetched page.
- **End screen and close (about 9:30).** Close on the viewer's key, the same image as the hook, with the one line "five heights, a very old trick, and a bargain". Hand them to the next video on screen in the final 5 seconds, not a recap.

First-hour checks (from the playbook): look at CTR and average view duration on the first Test & Compare result after 48 hours; if A and B are within noise, keep the one with better average view duration, not CTR alone, since the title promise must be paid off.
