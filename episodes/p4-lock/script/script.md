## 2. Act one: what the lock actually checks (~0:30 to ~2:20)

Here's what's inside. [none]
The part you turn is called the plug. It sits inside a fixed shell, and turning it is what moves the bolt. [2.1]
Drilled down through both of them is a row of small holes, usually five. [2.7]
In each hole sits a stack: a key pin at the bottom, a driver pin above it, and a spring on top pushing them both down. [2.2]
The line where the plug meets the shell is called the shear line. [2.3]
With no key in, the springs push every driver pin across that line, into the plug. The plug is pinned in place. It can't turn. [2.5]
Now slide the right key in. [none]
Each cut lifts its stack to a different height. And at the right height, the gap between the two pins lands exactly on the shear line. [2.2, 2.3]
All five gaps on the line at once, and the plug is free. [2.3]
That's the whole test. [1.2]
The pins never feel the outline of your key. Only how high it lifts them, at five points. [1.2]
The shape of the slot does a cruder check: it decides which blanks fit in at all. But the pins do the real work. [2.6]
Cut one position wrong, even slightly, and a pin stops short of the line, or pushes past it. [2.4, 1.3]
Either way, the plug stays locked. [2.4]
So the real secret isn't the key. It's a short list of numbers: how deep each cut goes. [1.2]
Makers choose from a handful of standard depths at each position. [2.8]
Multiply those choices together, and a lock can have anywhere from several thousand to several million different keys. [2.9]
Which sounds like your key is one of a kind. [none]
Except, sometimes, it isn't meant to be. [none]

## 3. The second key (~2:20 to ~3:30)

In a lot of buildings, offices, schools, apartment blocks, someone else carries a key that opens your door. And theirs opens every other door too. [1.4, 3.2]
It's called a master key. And the trick is tiny. [3.1]
In some of the stacks, there's an extra thin pin, a master pin. [3.1]
Now that stack has two places where it can split. Two heights that both work. [3.1]
Your key lines up one gap. The master key lines up the other. The lock can't tell the difference. [3.1, 1.2]
Systems come in levels. A master key opens a group of doors. A grand master opens several groups. [3.2]
But there's a catch. [none]
In two thousand and three, Matt Blaze, a computer security researcher then at AT and T Labs, studied master-keyed buildings. [3.4, 3.5]
Because each stack works on its own, the lock doesn't only accept two keys. It accepts mixes of the two, position by position. More keys than anyone planned. [3.3]
And he found the weakness in every system he tested, because it comes from the design itself. [3.5]
A lock that's happy with two answers is a lock that's easier to fool. [3.3]
Which brings us to the oldest claim about locks. [none]

## 4. Act two: a very old idea (~3:30 to ~4:50)

You've probably heard that the pin lock is four thousand years old, invented in ancient Egypt. [4.1]
It's repeated everywhere. And it's shaky. [4.1]
A historian traced that number back, and found it came from a nineteen fifty-seven lock catalogue that misread a scholar's work from the eighteen-eighties. [4.1]
And that scholar himself doubted the pin lock existed in the age of the pharaohs. [4.2]
What archaeologists have actually found is younger: wooden pin-lock bolts from Roman-era Egypt. [4.3]
Here's how they work. A wooden bolt with holes in it. Pegs drop into the holes and hold it shut. A wooden key with matching pins lifts the pegs, and the bolt slides free. [4.4]
Same principle as your front door. Lift every pin to the right height. [4.4, 1.2]
And it lasted. Locks like these were still in everyday use in Egypt and the Near East into the twentieth century. [4.5]
Then, in the eighteen-forties, an American lock maker named Linus Yale, a specialist in bank locks, patented a door lock with spring-loaded pins. [4.10, 4.15]
His son, also Linus Yale, had started out as a portrait painter. [4.8, 4.14]
In eighteen sixty-five, the son patented the design you carry today: a compact cylinder, opened by a small, flat steel key. [4.12, 2.11]
And here's the twist. Even that patent says one of its goals was making the lock harder to pick. [4.13]
Because by then, everyone knew what had happened in London. [none]

## 5. 1851: the unpickable lock (~4:50 to ~6:50)

In the seventeen-eighties, an English inventor named Joseph Bramah went into the lock business with a design he believed could not be picked. [5.4]
To prove it, a lock sat in the window of his London shop at a hundred and twenty-four Piccadilly, with a notice: two hundred guineas to anyone who could pick it or open it. [5.1]
It sat there for decades. [5.2]
Nobody claimed the money. [5.2]
Then came eighteen fifty-one, and the Great Exhibition: a show of the world's industry, in the Crystal Palace in Hyde Park, with around six million visits. [5.6]
Among the exhibitors was Alfred Charles Hobbs, an American locksmith from Boston, in London for a New York lock company. [5.5]
First, he opened a Chubb lock on a strongroom door in Westminster, in twenty-five minutes, in front of witnesses. [5.7]
Chubb's locks were famous. Their detector lock, patented in eighteen eighteen, was built to reveal anyone who had tampered with it. [5.8]
Then Hobbs went after the Bramah lock. [5.9]
Under the eye of three arbitrators, he worked on it for fifty-one hours, spread over sixteen days. [5.9, 5.10]
On the twenty-third of August, eighteen fifty-one, he showed it open. Undamaged. [5.9]
The arbitrators awarded him the two hundred guineas. [5.10]
Bramah's company protested. Nobody had watched him work, they said, and his tools were fixed to the woodwork in a way that wouldn't work on a real door. [5.11]
But the press called it the Great Lock Controversy, and Britain was rattled: an American had beaten the best of British locks. [5.13]
Neither of those locks was a pin lock, by the way. Bramah's used sliders, and Chubb's used levers. [5.3, 5.8]
But the lesson stuck to every lock that came after. [8.2]
There's no such thing as unpickable. [5.14, 8.2]
The only real question is: how long does it take? [8.1]

## 6. Act three: why your lock can be beaten at all (~6:50 to ~7:50)

So what is it about the pin lock, specifically? [none]
It isn't a secret trick. It's a manufacturing detail. [6.1]
Nothing is made perfectly. The holes in a real lock aren't drilled in a perfect line. [6.1]
So when the plug is pushed to turn, the pins don't all press at once. One pin catches before the others. [6.2]
And a lock that can be worked on one pin at a time is much weaker than one that has to be beaten all at once. [6.2]
That's the flaw, in principle. And that's as far as we'll go with it. [none]
It still takes real skill, and it's much harder against a well-made lock. [6.3]

## 7. The answers, and the twist (~7:50 to ~9:00)

So lock makers fight back. [none]
Some driver pins are shaped like spools, thinner in the middle, so they catch at the wrong moment and mislead anyone trying to set them. [7.1]
Others use tighter tolerances, more pins, trickier keyways, or a second locking bar that checks every pin in a different way. [7.2]
And in the US, lock hardware is graded, with grade one the toughest, mostly on how many times it can be used before it wears out. [7.3]
Pick resistance is tested separately. [7.4]
But here's the twist. [none]
Most burglars don't bother with any of this. [7.6, 7.7]
In US police figures for twenty nineteen, more than half of burglaries involved force. [7.6]
And in a US survey of victims, damaging or removing a door was the most common way in. [7.7]
Often, the lock never even came into it. Many burglars simply walked through a door or window that was unlocked. [7.7]
The lock on your door doesn't make you untouchable. [8.1]
It buys time. It's a bargain between cost, convenience and seconds. [8.1]
